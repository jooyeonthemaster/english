In a study at the University of Pittsburgh in 1998, researchers Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen conducted an experiment to explore the implications for the human brain’s perception of body ownership. The subjects sat with their left hand resting on a small table. A realistic rubber hand was placed on the table as well, and then the real hand was hidden from view by a standing screen. The subjects were told to focus on the rubber hand. Both the real hand and the rubber hand were then simultaneously stroked with a paintbrush. At some point, the researchers stopped using the paintbrush on the real hand. Even though only the rubber hand was being stroked, all of the subjects reported experiencing the sensation of the paintbrush. This phenomenon, now known as the “rubber hand illusion,” illustrates a fascinating aspect of the human brain’s perception of the body. It demonstrates that the concept of body ownershipcan extend to external objects. When the right conditions are met, there can be an illusionary feeling of sensory input from things that are not actually part of our physical structure.

In one of the most famous passages of Being and Nothingness, “The Look,” JeanPaul Sartre describes the peculiar vulnerability that develops when someone goes from seeing (being a self with a perspective on the world) to being seen (having to confront the perspective of another on one’s self). He illustrates it with the example of someone looking through a keyhole who suddenly finds himself caught by someone watching him. The look of the other is always unnerving, Sartre argues, not only because we momentarily recognize ourselves in it through our imagination of their judgment of us but also because we don’t. We can always step back, challenge our perception of others’ perceptions of ourselves, or explain them away — but we don’t know what these perceptions really are. Others have the distinctive power of making us feel judged in ways we cannot fully control. Social life is all about the fear that accompanies our awareness that we can never access what the other sees. We can only guess.
