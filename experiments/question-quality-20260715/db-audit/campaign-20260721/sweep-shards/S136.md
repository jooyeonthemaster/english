Our cognitive system is built around scarcity. At any given moment, the senses are flooded with
far more information than the brain can process, and so attention functions as a gatekeeper,
admitting only what seems relevant. This filtering is not neutral. What we attend to is shaped by
prior expectations, emotional states, and even the language we speak. A driver focused on finding a

The Function of Boredom

Boredom is often treated as a problem to be solved, especially in an age when distraction is
always within reach. But some researchers argue that the discomfort of having nothing to do may
serve a vital psychological purpose. When the mind is unoccupied, it tends to wander, drifting
through memories, anxieties, and half-formed ideas. This wandering, far from being wasted time, is
associated with creativity and self-reflection; many writers and scientists have reported that their
best insights arrived in moments of apparent idleness. The constant availability of stimulation may
therefore come at a hidden cost. By filling every empty interval with content, we may be
eliminating the very conditions under which new thinking takes shape. Boredom, in this view, is
less a failure of life to be interesting than a doorway the mind must occasionally pass through if it is
to arrive somewhere genuinely new.
