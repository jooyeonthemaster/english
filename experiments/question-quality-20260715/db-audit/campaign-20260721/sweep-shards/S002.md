Moral concern tends to weaken with distance. We are more disturbed by suffering we can see than by suffering reported from far away, more troubled by a single identifiable victim than by anonymous statistics describing thousands. This pattern is not a moral judgment but a feature of human psychology, shaped over countless generations by ancestors whose survival depended on caring intensely for those nearby. Every technological convenience carries hidden costs that often go unnoticed until much later. The automobile, celebrated as liberation from distance, gradually reshaped cities around its needs, pushing pedestrians to the margins and demanding vast expanses of asphalt. The smartphone, marketed as a tool for connection, has been linked to declining attention spans and rising loneliness. None of this means that the technologies themselves were mistakes; their benefits are genuine and considerable. But the deeper lesson is that we tend to evaluate new tools by the problems they solve while ignoring the new problems they create. The result is a culture that rushes to adopt before it has paused to consider. A more mature relationship with technology would treat each innovation not as an unalloyed gain but as a trade, and would ask honestly what we are exchanging in return for what we receive.
