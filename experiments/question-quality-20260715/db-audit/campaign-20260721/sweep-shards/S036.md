the human immune system mounts a defense of remarkable sophistication, one whose choreography rivals that of any organized army. When a pathogen breaches the epithelial barrier, resident macrophages act as first responders, engulfing the intruder while releasing cytokines—chemical distress signals that summon reinforcements to the site of infection. Wave after wave, the intruder is surrounded, neutralized, and cleared.

What distinguishes this system from any conventional army, however, is its memory. Once the invader is vanquished, a reserve of memory cells persists for years, silently patrolling the body. Should the same pathogen ever return, it is met not with confusion, but with a retaliation so swift and overwhelming that the infection is extinguished before symptoms can even arise. It is precisely this principle—defense through remembered experience—that every vaccine is designed to exploit.
