The GravityLight
Kerosene-burning lamps provide light throughout the developing world. However, these lamps are targets for replacement because the fumes generated by burning kerosene in close quarters are a major health problem. A seemingly simple solution is GravityLight, developed by deciwatt.org. To power the device, a user fills an included bag with about 20 pounds of rock or dirt. He or she attaches it to the cord hanging down from the device and lifts it upward. The potential energy stored in that lifting motion is then gradually converted to electricity by the GravityLight. It slowly lets the bag downward over the course of about 30 minutes and powers a light or other electrical device during that time. It’s currently priced at about $10. Because it requires no cost to run, the development team estimates that the cost to buy it will be paid back in about three months by saving the money used to buy kerosene.

They were both discovered around the same time. In 1928, the Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming discovered that his molds of Penicillium produced an antibiotic. Just over a decade later, Australian pharmacologist Howard Florey purified this antibiotic into usable amounts, and in 1944, chemical engineer Margaret Hutchinson Rousseau was able to put it into production as the antibiotic penicillin.
