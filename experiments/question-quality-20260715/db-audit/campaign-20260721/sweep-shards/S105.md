A red herring fallacy occurs when an arguer subtly changes the subject of the argument or focuses on an irrelevant aspect of an argument. If, for example, a school board is voting on whether to cut high school football coaches' salaries, it is a red herring to say that voting in favor of such a cut is a vote against education. It is true that the measure might reduce the education budget, but that is a far cry from the claim that cutting coaches' salaries is anti-education. It is not clear what "educational" purposes football coaches serve, or whether reducing their salaries would have a corrosive effect on education. It is just as likely that football itself has a corrosive effect on education, and therefore, should be cut altogether. In fact, the money removed from that budget line might be earmarked for even better educational programs, teacher salaries, books, digital resources, or student health. The problem is that red herrings introduce information that is irrelevant to whether we should believe or act on some claim. ─> A red herring fallacy distracts from the main issue by introducing unrelated points, such as claiming that cutting high school football coaches' salaries equates to undermining education.
