```
Our cognitive system is built around scarcity. At any given moment, the senses are flooded with far more information than the brain can process, and so attention functions as a gatekeeper, admitting only what seems relevant. This filtering is not neutral. What we attend to is shaped by prior expectations, emotional states, and even the language we speak. A driver focused on finding a parking spot may fail to notice a friend waving from the sidewalk; a scientist trained to observe certain patterns may overlook anomalies that contradict her theory. In this sense, perception is less a passive reception of the world than an active negotiation with it. The implication is unsettling: two observers placed in identical environments may experience meaningfully different realities. Recognizing the constructed nature of attention does not free us from its limits, but it can make us more humble about what we claim to have seen.
```

```
For much of the twentieth century, evolutionary theory was dominated by the image of nature as a battlefield, with selfish genes competing ruthlessly for survival. More recent research, however, suggests that cooperation has been at least as important as competition in shaping life on Earth. Cells cooperate to form organisms; organisms cooperate to form colonies; species cooperate across boundaries in mutually beneficial relationships known as symbiosis. Even within a single body, trillions of microorganisms quietly perform tasks essential to our digestion and immunity. What appears to be a solitary creature is, on closer inspection, an alliance of countless smaller agents. ## P12  [passage] cmp0y72020001jy04ckfo0zwu
```
title: The Sunk Cost Fallacy
```
Few mistakes in reasoning are as common as the tendency to throw good money after bad. Economists call it the sunk cost fallacy: the belief that past investments justify future commitments, even when the future looks dim. A factory that has spent millions developing a doomed product will often keep developing it; a moviegoer who has paid for a ticket will often sit through a film he is no longer enjoying. Rationally, the past is irretrievable, and decisions should be based only on expected future outcomes. Emotionally, however, we resist the idea that earlier sacrifices were in vain. To walk away feels like an admission of failure, while persisting preserves the illusion that the original choice was sound. Wise decision-making, then, requires a peculiar kind of courage: the willingness to treat sunk costs as exactly what they are, sunk and irrelevant, regardless of how much they once mattered.
```
